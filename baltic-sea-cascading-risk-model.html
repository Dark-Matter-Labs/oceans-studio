The Baltic Sea’s risk cascades, read from the sea.

In the Baltic the cascade runs through the deep water. A century of nutrients from a catchment four times the sea’s area has left the deep basins without oxygen, anoxia releases the phosphorus that sustains it, and cod now spawn in one basin of the three they once used, held open by hydrography rather than management. The basin has targets with weak figures beneath them, a fast lever proven only at the coast, and a largest capital event — 32 GW of offshore wind refused — decided on defence advice that no ecological indicator can see.

Preface · status and method

A basin model, graded link by link.

This is an indicative risk model for one sea. It renders the Baltic Register — 12 keystone systems, five forcing nodes, ten receptor systems, and 20 graded transmission edges — and extends the cascade through the water column, the coast, the seabed, and the institutions that measure and decide, from published assessments and research. The catchment spans 14 countries, so the model is bounded by the catchment rather than the coastline. It contains no new data, modelling, or fieldwork: every finding is a reading of published sources, and where the evidence is thin the model says so.

The next stage is social and technical validation. People who work at and with the sea, scientific and regional bodies, and those developing ways to represent marine life in decisions need to test whether this account matches their knowledge and whose risks are missing. Technical review must examine mechanisms, spatial connections, timing, and magnitudes, with the standard of evidence proportionate to the consequence and reversibility of the decisions it informs.

The object of risk

What is at risk is the sea’s continuing work.

The Baltic holds oxygen in deep water that is renewed only by rare inflows, passes energy from plankton through sprat and herring to cod, seals, and seabirds, keeps coastal bays in the predator-dominated state that supports pike and perch, and buries in its sediments the nutrients its catchment sends. The coasts, fleets, ports, and cities around it draw on this work continuously without registering it. In the language of optionality it is slack: the margin that lets a shock be absorbed without forcing an immediate, costly choice.

Here much of that slack is already spent. Anoxic sediments return phosphorus instead of burying it, cod spawn in one basin instead of three, and stickleback-dominated bays lock out the predators that would restore them. If the next warm, still summer or acute spill arrives before those return, it meets a sea with fewer reserves, fewer living sources, and fewer responses still feasible.

Three distinctions organise the analysis. Compound stress means pressures occur together. A cascade requires one change to worsen another through a demonstrable mechanism. A feedback returns the consequence to an earlier source of vulnerability. A warm, still summer can deepen hypoxia and spread a cyanobacterial bloom at once without either causing the other.

Why the risk is basin-scale.

Water

One enclosed sea, rarely renewed.

The Baltic exchanges water with the North Sea only through the Danish straits, and its deep basins are ventilated by rare major inflows, the last in late 2014. A permanent halocline separates a fresher surface from the deep water, so what sinks below it stays.

Land

The catchment is four times the sea.

Fourteen countries and 85–90 million people drain into it. Soils, groundwater, and sediments store a century of surplus nutrients and release them for decades after abatement, so the basin’s condition is set upstream and years earlier.

Institutions

One convention, meeting informally.

Nine coastal states and the EU are parties to the Helsinki Convention. Russia remains one, but since March 2022 formal meetings involving it have been postponed, and the other nine parties work through informal sessions. Shared forcing creates correlation; a shared catchment, shared stocks, and shared shipping lanes create transmission.

Holdability

Which conditions are still holdable, at what cost, over what time.

The register’s keystone systems, read for capital: whether the objective is still reachable, whether the system shows hysteresis, how long an intervention takes to show, what holding it costs where a price exists, and how well it is observed. One position has a defensible unit cost — SEK 500–1,300 per kg of phosphorus over 20 years, from a single coastal demonstration — and the others do not. The value of maintenance already performed shows only against a counterfactual: had mid-1980s loads continued, the Baltic Proper would today have 82% larger oxygen-free bottom areas, 104% more wintertime inorganic nitrogen, and 58% more inorganic phosphorus.

Three keystones have no direct maintenance option: cod, the pelagic forage complex, and benthic habitat can be held only by maintaining the systems beneath them or by foregoing extraction. Market capital can carry three positions unaided — mussel farming, cyprinid removal, and noise mitigation, the last only because consent requires it.

The implication · regime-shift risk

When the sea loses the capacity to renew itself.

Stronger pressures meet thinner buffers. In the Baltic this is more than a conditional implication: a regime shift in deep-water oxygen in 1999 has persisted since, and three systems carry demonstrated hysteresis. What remains to be established is the cause of the eastern cod collapse, and whether the fast lever works at basin scale.

  1. Pressure intensifies

    Drivers impose harder conditions.

    Inputs still exceed the allowance in the Baltic Proper and the Gulf of Finland, bottom water runs warm, and a shipping lane carrying an uninsured fleet runs the length of the sea. Most of the nitrogen reduction to date came from the air, a seam that is finite.

  2. Buffering contracts

    Less remains to absorb the pressure.

    Oxygenated deep volume, spawning basins, coastal predators, and seabed fauna grow thinner. Each buffer serves a specific function: one surviving spawning basin cannot hold a stock that once used three, and a bay whose pike larvae are eaten does not restock itself.

  3. Recovery overlap

    Losses outrun replenishment.

    The clocks are long. Improvement becomes visible 1–2 decades after loads fall and targets take about 50 years; coastal sediment ammonium stays elevated for at least 180 years, and indefinitely under present loading. Only at the coast do measures show within years.

  4. Consequences return

    Damage weakens the next recovery.

    Anoxia releases the phosphorus that sustains it; nitrogen fixers refill what reduction removes; clupeid dominance holds cod down; sticklebacks eat the larvae of the predators that would displace them; measurement gaps keep maintenance unpriced. Each return is only as established as its weakest link.

  5. Connected coasts

    Several states lose support together.

    Nine states share one deep water below one halocline. The same shipping route runs from the Danish straits to the Gulf of Finland, the same catchment spans 14 countries, and the same data gaps sit on the Russian side of the sea, where they predate 2022.

Possible systemic consequence

A persistent change in how the Baltic functions.

The risk is that the deep water’s capacity to bury nutrients, the food web’s capacity to carry energy to cod, and the coast’s capacity to hold predator-dominated bays stay lost together, each loss sustaining the others. Three regime-shift claims are demonstrated here: deep-water oxygen, through an internal phosphorus source of about 2.3 g per m² a year; western cod, held in a novel low-productivity state by warming; and coastal fish, where 486 bays show bimodal states. The eastern cod productivity collapse has no established cause, and the oxygen explanation is contradicted.

A disturbance within a renewing sea

Functions are interrupted, but enough oxygen, spawning volume, predators, and seabed fauna remain to rebuild them. The next season begins with replenished capacity.

A possible shift in the sea itself

The means of renewal are repeatedly lost. Even a cool, windy year leaves a phosphorus-releasing seabed, one spawning basin, and stickleback bays. The starting conditions for recovery have changed.

Slow recovery alone does not prove a tipping point. Persistent external pressure, an abrupt transition, and a self-maintaining alternative state are distinct explanations to test. No government document in the five states examined takes irreversibility as its principal framing.

What makes a cascade

Connections become more consequential as buffers thin.

The sea and the systems that depend on it were always connected. The testable question is whether transmission is becoming stronger, more synchronous, more persistent, or harder to absorb.

Order and recurrence

Different sequences can produce the same loss of function.

The risk runs through several event-led stories. These routes can operate separately or converge; their interaction may change the magnitude or duration of loss, and additional amplification must be demonstrated rather than presumed.

Latency

The basin runs on a fifty-year clock; the coast on a faster one.

Latency is the field a maintenance argument most depends on. The Baltic has one authoritative basin figure and a range of published estimates; at the coast, measures show within years. Three clocks run separately: the time to the next consequential exposure, the time for an intervention to show, and the time to make action feasible.

Where authorities disagree

Disagreement is recorded as a finding.

Where authorities reach opposite conclusions, both are recorded and the disagreement is treated as a result. One sits on a contested link in the map — whether bottom trawling drives hypoxia — and decides whether a trawl closure is a nutrient measure or only a fisheries measure.

The confidence layer

Targets exist; the figures beneath them are weak.

The Baltic has management targets, with weak measurement confidence beneath them. No Baltic state reports uncertainty on its nutrient sources, and only three report partly on flow and load. Four of the five highest-load states use monthly-mean load methods that HELCOM itself says run low. Where a state does not quantify scattered dwellings, stormwater, or deposition on inland waters, the residual usually read as agriculture is biased high, so national agricultural shares are not comparable between states, and any chart placing them side by side is an artefact. The basin’s single largest phosphorus figure rests on proxy-year data.

Measurement is thinnest where the sea is failing. The Gulf of Finland, home to the largest claimed wastewater success, carries about 17% uncertainty on phosphorus and the highest nitrogen uncertainty of the seven basins. Soft-bottom fauna has no reliable threshold in the Gulf of Finland or the Gotland basins; the Gulf of Riga has no deposition stations; coastal fish are monitored in 32 of 42 units; and Russia’s zone is excluded from the regional ecosystem model for lack of data.

Cross-border flows are partly observed and partly negotiated. Narva discharge has been estimated since 2015; Daugava loads are apportioned by catchment share; a fifth of the Nemunas, diverted to Kaliningrad, goes unmonitored; and the German share of the Oder is a fixed constant of 3.7% of nitrogen and 8.5% of phosphorus, with 1,804 km² of Czech catchment omitted. What fraction of the basin’s nutrient figure is observed cannot yet be stated. These gaps mostly predate 2022: what changed in March 2022 was the institutional machinery, not the observations, so they will not close when the war does. Satellite and model syntheses preserve continuity for physics and surface chlorophyll, but cannot create a measured river flux, a benthic sample, or a deep-water profile.

Two flagship outputs of one Swedish research programme, both from October 2024, report that the central pressure–state link in their domain cannot be demonstrated with available data: hypoxia does not explain the cod collapse, and trawling does not explain benthic condition. The cumulative-assessment apparatus beneath them is additive and expert-weighted, and cannot represent interactions.

The vocabulary is itself a finding. Thresholds are used extensively in every state examined, always as compliance lines. No 2024 or later government document takes irreversibility or alternative stable states as its principal framing; in Poland the idea appears in institutional language outside the formal assessment, not inside it. The assessment system can report that a threshold was failed. It has no apparatus for reporting that a threshold has closed behind it.

Calendar

The decisions that set the next cycle.

Every state examined is running measures written against the previous cycle’s assessment, and the body deciding the post-2030 architecture cannot formally convene: Russia remains a Contracting Party, and since March 2022 the other nine parties have worked through informal sessions.

What the evidence does not yet show.
